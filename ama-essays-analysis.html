AMA Essays: In Depth Analysis

A comprehensive guide to American Medical Association style—citation formatting, manuscript structure, ethical writing, and medical publication standards

Introduction

The American Medical Association (AMA) Manual of Style is the authoritative reference for medical and scientific publishing. First published in 1962, now in its 11th edition (2020), it provides comprehensive guidelines for manuscript preparation, citation formatting, ethical considerations, and scientific communication in medicine and related fields.

This analysis examines the core principles of AMA style, citation mechanics, manuscript structure, ethical guidelines, common errors, and connections to broader scientific writing practices. Mastery of AMA style is essential for medical students, researchers, clinicians, and health science professionals publishing in peer-reviewed journals.

Historical Context & Purpose

Evolution of AMA Style

  • 1962 - 1st Edition: Initial codification of JAMA (Journal of the American Medical Association) editorial standards.
  • 1970s-90s: Expanded to cover statistical reporting, ethical guidelines, conflict of interest disclosure.
  • 2000s: Digital publishing considerations, online citation formats, multimedia content.
  • 2020 - 11th Edition: Updated for inclusive language, data transparency, preprint servers, ORCID identifiers, sex and gender terminology.

Why AMA Style Matters

Medical literature requires precision, clarity, reproducibility, and ethical integrity. AMA style ensures:

  • Standardization: Consistent formatting across thousands of medical journals.
  • Traceability: Accurate citations enable verification and further research.
  • Transparency: Disclosure of funding, conflicts of interest, ethical approvals.
  • Accessibility: Clear presentation of complex medical data for diverse audiences.
  • Integrity: Guidelines prevent plagiarism, ghost authorship, data fabrication.

Core Principles of AMA Style

Citation Philosophy

AMA uses a superscript numerical citation system (citation-sequence or citation-name). Each source receives a number at first mention; subsequent citations use the same number. This differs from author-date systems (APA) and allows dense citation without disrupting text flow.

Key Characteristics

  • Superscript Numbers: In-text citations appear as superscript (e.g., "Several studies1-3 have shown...").
  • Reference List Order: Numerical order by first appearance in text.
  • Author Format: Last name followed by initials without periods (Smith JA, not Smith, J.A.).
  • Abbreviations: Journal titles abbreviated per PubMed/Index Medicus standards.
  • DOI Required: Digital Object Identifiers (DOI) preferred over URLs when available.

Citation Formatting: Detailed Rules

In-Text Citations

Single Source:

Recent research1 indicates improved outcomes with early intervention.

Multiple Sources (consecutive):

Several meta-analyses4-7 support this conclusion.

Multiple Sources (non-consecutive):

These findings2,5,9 align with current guidelines.

Author Named in Text:

Smith and colleagues3 reported significant improvement in patient outcomes.

Journal Article (Standard Format)

Format:

Author(s). Article title. Journal Abbrev. Year;volume(issue):page range. doi:XX.XXXX/xxxxx

Example:

1. Johnson KE, Williams RT, Martinez DE. Effect of metformin on cardiovascular outcomes in type 2 diabetes. JAMA. 2023;329(8):645-654. doi:10.1001/jama.2023.1234

Key Journal Citation Rules

  • Author Names: List up to 6 authors (Last Name Initials); if >6, list first 3 followed by "et al."
  • Article Title: Sentence case (only first word and proper nouns capitalized).
  • Journal Title: Abbreviated per NLM catalog (JAMA, N Engl J Med, Lancet).
  • Year: Publication year only (not month unless no volume/issue).
  • Volume/Issue: Volume in regular font; issue in parentheses.
  • Page Range: First and last page (645-654, not 645-54).
  • DOI: Include when available; omit "https://doi.org/" prefix.

Book Citation

Format:

Author(s). Book Title. Edition (if not 1st). Publisher; Year.

Example:

2. Harrison TR, Fauci AS, Kasper DL, et al. Harrison's Principles of Internal Medicine. 21st ed. McGraw-Hill; 2022.

Book Chapter

Format:

Chapter Author(s). Chapter title. In: Book Editor(s), eds. Book Title. Edition. Publisher; Year:page range.

Example:

3. Thompson AE, Liu Y. Pharmacological management of hypertension. In: Chen MH, Davis KL, eds. Clinical Cardiology: A Companion to Braunwald's Heart Disease. 5th ed. Elsevier; 2023:112-145.

Website/Online Source

Format:

Author(s)/Organization. Title. Website name. Published date. Updated date. Accessed date. URL

Example:

4. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. COVID-19 vaccination schedule. CDC website. Updated January 15, 2024. Accessed January 20, 2024. https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/covid-19/schedule

Preprint (arXiv, medRxiv, bioRxiv)

Example:

5. Chen L, Rodriguez M, Patel S. Efficacy of novel CRISPR therapy in sickle cell disease. Preprint posted online December 3, 2023. medRxiv. doi:10.1101/2023.12.03.23299456

Manuscript Structure for Medical Papers

IMRaD Format

Most medical research articles follow IMRaD structure: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion.

Title Page

  • Title: Concise, informative, descriptive (≤150 characters including spaces).
  • Authors: Full names, degrees, affiliations, ORCID IDs.
  • Corresponding Author: Contact information (email, phone).
  • Word Count: Abstract and main text separately.
  • Funding Sources: Grant numbers, sponsors.
  • Conflicts of Interest: Financial disclosures, industry relationships.

Abstract

Structured abstract (200-300 words) with subheadings:

  • Objective/Background: Research question, rationale.
  • Methods: Study design, participants, interventions, measures.
  • Results: Primary findings with key statistics.
  • Conclusions: Interpretation, clinical implications.

Introduction

Brief background, literature review, knowledge gap, specific aims/hypotheses. Typically 2-4 paragraphs. Conclude with clear statement of study purpose.

Methods

Detailed, reproducible description of study procedures:

  • Study Design: Randomized controlled trial, cohort study, case-control, etc.
  • Participants: Inclusion/exclusion criteria, recruitment, sample size calculation.
  • Interventions/Exposures: Detailed protocols, dosing, timing.
  • Outcomes: Primary and secondary endpoints, measurement tools.
  • Statistical Analysis: Tests used, significance level, software.
  • Ethics: IRB/ethics committee approval, informed consent, trial registration (ClinicalTrials.gov).

Results

Present findings in logical sequence without interpretation. Use tables/figures to display data efficiently. Report statistics with precision: mean (SD), median (IQR), 95% CI, P values.

  • Participant Flow: Enrollment, allocation, follow-up, analysis (CONSORT diagram for trials).
  • Baseline Characteristics: Demographics, clinical features (Table 1).
  • Primary Outcomes: Main results with effect sizes, confidence intervals.
  • Secondary Outcomes: Additional analyses, subgroups, adverse events.

Discussion

Interpret findings in context of existing literature:

  • Principal Findings: Summarize key results and their significance.
  • Comparison to Literature: How do findings align/contrast with prior studies?
  • Mechanisms/Explanations: Biological plausibility, theoretical frameworks.
  • Strengths & Limitations: Study design advantages, potential biases, generalizability.
  • Clinical Implications: Relevance to practice, policy recommendations.
  • Future Research: Unanswered questions, next steps.

Conclusion

Brief paragraph summarizing main findings and their importance. Avoid overstating results or claiming causality from observational data.

Tables and Figures

Tables

  • Numbering: Arabic numerals in order of text mention (Table 1, Table 2).
  • Title: Descriptive, appears above table.
  • Footnotes: Abbreviations, statistical symbols (*, †, ‡), P values explained below table.
  • Formatting: Minimal horizontal lines; no vertical lines; align decimal points.
  • Data Precision: Report appropriate significant figures (avoid false precision).

Figures

  • Types: Graphs, charts, photographs, illustrations, flowcharts.
  • Quality: High resolution (300 dpi minimum for publication).
  • Legend: Descriptive caption below figure; define all symbols, abbreviations, arrows.
  • Color: Use colorblind-friendly palettes; ensure grayscale legibility.
  • Permissions: Obtain copyright permission for republished figures; acknowledge source.

When to Use Tables vs. Figures

  • Tables: Precise values, multiple variables, complex comparisons.
  • Figures: Trends, relationships, distributions, spatial/temporal patterns.
  • Avoid Redundancy: Don't present same data in text, table, AND figure.

Ethical Considerations in Medical Writing

Authorship Criteria (ICMJE Guidelines)

All authors must meet ALL four criteria:

  1. Substantial contributions to conception/design, data acquisition, or analysis/interpretation.
  2. Drafting the work or revising it critically for important intellectual content.
  3. Final approval of the version to be published.
  4. Agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work.

Non-authors: Those who contributed but don't meet criteria (technical assistance, funding acquisition only) are acknowledged, not listed as authors.

Conflicts of Interest (COI)

Disclose any financial, professional, or personal relationships that could influence research:

  • Financial: Consulting fees, stock ownership, patents, speaker honoraria from industry.
  • Professional: Employment, advisory roles, expert testimony.
  • Personal: Family relationships with industry representatives.
  • Institutional: University/hospital contracts with pharmaceutical companies.

Plagiarism & Self-Plagiarism

  • Plagiarism: Using others' words/ideas without attribution.严格禁止. Use quotes, paraphrases, and citations.
  • Self-Plagiarism: Republishing your own work without disclosure (duplicate publication). Acceptable if clearly indicated (e.g., translated versions, conference proceedings to full article).
  • Text Recycling: Reusing Methods sections is sometimes acceptable if disclosed; Results/Discussion must be original.

Patient Privacy (HIPAA Compliance)

  • De-identification: Remove all 18 HIPAA identifiers (names, dates, MRNs, photos showing faces).
  • Case Reports: Obtain written patient consent; alter non-essential details to protect identity.
  • Images: Mask faces or obtain explicit permission; blur tattoos, scars if identifiable.

Data Integrity

  • Fabrication: Making up data—严格禁止, grounds for retraction and career termination.
  • Falsification: Manipulating data/images to misrepresent results—equally严重.
  • Cherry-Picking: Selective reporting of favorable outcomes while hiding negative results—publication bias.
  • Data Sharing: Increasingly required by journals; deposit data in repositories (ClinicalTrials.gov results, Dryad, Figshare).

Common Errors & How to Avoid Them

Citation Mistakes

  • ❌ Wrong: Using periods in author initials (Smith, J.A.)
    ✅ Correct: Smith JA
  • ❌ Wrong: Full journal names (Journal of the American Medical Association)
    ✅ Correct: Abbreviated (JAMA)
  • ❌ Wrong: Title case in article titles (Effect Of Metformin On Outcomes)
    ✅ Correct: Sentence case (Effect of metformin on outcomes)
  • ❌ Wrong: Missing DOI when available
    ✅ Correct: Always include DOI for journal articles
  • ❌ Wrong: Listing all 15 authors
    ✅ Correct: First 3 authors, then "et al." if >6 total

Statistical Reporting Errors

  • P-Value Precision: Report as P = .03, not P < .05 (unless P < .001).
  • Confidence Intervals: Always report with effect sizes (RR 1.8; 95% CI, 1.2-2.7).
  • Statistical vs. Clinical Significance: P < .05 doesn't mean clinically meaningful.
  • Multiple Comparisons: Adjust P values (Bonferroni, FDR) to avoid false positives.

Writing Style Issues

  • Passive Voice Overuse: Medical writing traditionally passive, but modern preference for active voice when clear.
  • Jargon: Define abbreviations at first use; avoid excessive acronyms.
  • Wordiness: Be concise—"In order to" → "To"; "Due to the fact that" → "Because".
  • Ambiguous Pronouns: "It was found that it improved outcomes" → specify subjects clearly.

Inclusive Language

  • Sex vs. Gender: Sex = biological (male/female); Gender = identity (man/woman/nonbinary).
  • Person-First Language: "Patients with diabetes" not "diabetics"; "Person with schizophrenia" not "schizophrenic".
  • Race/Ethnicity: Capitalize (Black, White, Hispanic); explain relevance to research question.
  • Disability: "Person with disability" not "disabled person"; avoid "suffers from" (judgmental).

Specialized Medical Writing Formats

Systematic Review & Meta-Analysis

Comprehensive synthesis of all available evidence on a research question. Follow PRISMA guidelines:

  • Protocol: Pre-register search strategy, inclusion criteria (PROSPERO).
  • Search: Multiple databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane), grey literature.
  • Screening: Two independent reviewers, conflicts resolved by third.
  • Data Extraction: Standardized forms, risk of bias assessment.
  • Meta-Analysis: Pool effect sizes, forest plots, heterogeneity testing (I²).
  • GRADE: Rate quality of evidence (high, moderate, low, very low).

Case Report

Detailed description of unique patient presentation, diagnosis, or treatment:

  • Introduction: Brief background on condition, why case is novel.
  • Case Presentation: Patient demographics (de-identified), symptoms, timeline, physical exam, labs, imaging.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Alternative explanations considered.
  • Treatment & Outcomes: Interventions, response, follow-up.
  • Discussion: Lessons learned, literature review, clinical pearls.

Clinical Practice Guideline

Evidence-based recommendations for clinical decision-making:

  • Scope: Define clinical questions (PICO format: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome).
  • Evidence Review: Systematic literature search, quality appraisal.
  • Recommendations: Strength (strong/weak) and quality of evidence (high/low).
  • Implementation: Practical guidance, patient decision aids, quality measures.

Letter to the Editor / Correspondence

  • Purpose: Comment on published article, present brief original data, discuss emerging issues.
  • Format: Short (typically 400-600 words), focused, referenced.
  • Tone: Professional, constructive; avoid personal attacks.

Journal Submission Process

Choosing a Journal

  • Scope & Audience: Match journal's focus to your topic (general medicine vs. specialty).
  • Impact Factor: Journal Citation Reports (JCR); higher IF = wider readership but more competitive.
  • Open Access: Immediate free access vs. subscription; article processing charges (APCs).
  • Predatory Journals: Avoid low-quality, pay-to-publish scams (check Beall's List, Think. Check. Submit.).

Peer Review

  1. Initial Screening: Editor checks fit, quality, ethics; ~30-50% desk rejected.
  2. Reviewer Assignment: 2-3 experts in field (single-blind, double-blind, or open review).
  3. Review: Evaluate methods, validity, significance, writing; recommend accept/revise/reject.
  4. Decision: Accept (rare on first submission), Minor Revision, Major Revision, Reject.
  5. Revision: Address all reviewer comments; resubmit with point-by-point response letter.
  6. Publication: Copyediting, proofs, online ahead of print, final issue.

Responding to Reviewers

  • Be Respectful: Thank reviewers for time and insights.
  • Address Every Comment: Point-by-point response; explain changes or rebuttals.
  • Revise Thoroughly: Don't just tweak wording—improve substance.
  • Highlight Changes: Track changes or bold text in revised manuscript.
  • Disagree Politely: If you disagree, provide evidence/rationale, not defensiveness.

Software & Tools for AMA Writing

Reference Managers

  • Zotero: Free, open-source, browser integration, AMA citation style plugin.
  • Mendeley: Free, PDF annotation, citation insertion (Word/LibreOffice).
  • EndNote: Paid, institutional licenses common, robust library management.
  • PubMed: Export citations directly to reference managers (MEDLINE format).

Writing & Collaboration

  • Microsoft Word: Standard for medical journals; track changes for co-authors.
  • Google Docs: Real-time collaboration, version history, cloud-based.
  • Overleaf (LaTeX): For complex equations, formatting; some journals accept LaTeX submissions.
  • Grammarly / ProWritingAid: Grammar/style checking (medical writing add-ons available).

Statistical Software

  • R / RStudio: Free, powerful, reproducible analyses, publication-quality plots.
  • SPSS: User-friendly GUI, common in clinical research.
  • Stata: Epidemiology focus, survival analysis, survey data.
  • GraphPad Prism: Biostatistics, easy graphs for basic analyses.

Literature Search

  • PubMed / MEDLINE: Primary database for biomedical literature (free via NIH).
  • Embase: More comprehensive than PubMed for pharmacology, European journals.
  • Cochrane Library: Systematic reviews, clinical trials registry.
  • Google Scholar: Broad coverage, citation tracking, full-text access via institutional logins.

Practical Exercises

Exercise 1: Format This Journal Citation

Given: Article by Sarah M. Johnson, David K. Lee, and Maria Rodriguez titled "Impact of telemedicine on rural healthcare access" published in The New England Journal of Medicine, volume 388, issue 12, pages 1098-1107, March 23, 2023, DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa2215678.

Your Answer:

Johnson SM, Lee DK, Rodriguez M. Impact of telemedicine on rural healthcare access. N Engl J Med. 2023;388(12):1098-1107. doi:10.1056/NEJMoa2215678

Exercise 2: In-Text Citation Practice

Insert superscript citations: "Multiple studies have demonstrated the efficacy of statins in reducing cardiovascular events. [cite sources 5, 8, and 12] However, recent meta-analyses [cite sources 15-18] suggest benefits may be overstated in low-risk populations."

Answer:

Multiple studies5,8,12 have demonstrated the efficacy of statins in reducing cardiovascular events. However, recent meta-analyses15-18 suggest benefits may be overstated in low-risk populations.

Exercise 3: Identify the Errors

Smith, J.A., Jones, B.C., Williams, D.E., et al. The Effect Of Early Intervention On Patient Outcomes. Journal of the American Medical Association. 2023; Vol. 329(8): pp. 645-654.

Errors:

  • Periods in initials (should be: Smith JA)
  • Title case in article title (should be: sentence case)
  • Full journal name (should be: JAMA)
  • Unnecessary "Vol." and "pp."
  • Periods after initials in author list
  • Missing DOI

  • AMA Manual of Style, 11th Edition (2020): Comprehensive 1,200+ page reference—print or online subscription.
  • ICMJE Recommendations: International Committee of Medical Journal Editors guidelines (free online).
  • CONSORT Statement: Standards for reporting randomized trials.
  • PRISMA Guidelines: Standards for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
  • STROBE Statement: Standards for observational epidemiology studies.

  • DOI Resolver: Convert DOIs to full citations (https://doi.org).
  • ORCID: Register for persistent researcher identifier (https://orcid.org).
  • Crossref Similarity Check: Plagiarism detection via iThenticate (journal submissions).

Writing Guides

  • "Writing for Biomedical Publication" (University of Manchester)—free online course.
  • "How to Write a Paper" (Hall GM, ed.)—practical guide for medical writers.
  • "Scientific Writing and Communication" (Angelika Hofmann)—covers papers, grants, presentations.

Journals for Practice

  • JAMA, NEJM, Lancet: Top-tier general medicine journals using AMA style.
  • Specialty Journals: Check "Instructions for Authors" for AMA variations.
  • Open Access Journals: PLOS Medicine, BMJ Open—free to read, APC to publish.

Connections to Other Citation Styles

  • APA Style: Author-date system (Smith, 2023); common in psychology, nursing, public health. More author emphasis in text.
  • MLA Style: Author-page system (Smith 45); humanities focus, less common in medicine.
  • Vancouver Style: Numerical like AMA; used by some medical journals (BMJ, Lancet variations).
  • Chicago/Turabian: Footnotes or author-date; historical/qualitative research in medicine.
  • CSE Style: Council of Science Editors; three systems (citation-sequence, name-year, citation-name) for broader sciences.

Understanding AMA provides foundation for adapting to other medical/scientific styles. Core principles—accuracy, attribution, ethics—remain constant across all systems.
