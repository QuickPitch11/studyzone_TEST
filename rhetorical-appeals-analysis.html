Rhetorical Appeals: In Depth Analysis

In-depth analytic guide to identifying and using rhetorical appeals for persuasive writing and speaking.

What Are Rhetorical Appeals?

Rhetorical appeals are strategies speakers and writers use to persuade an audience. Classical rhetoric identifies three primary appeals:

  • Ethos — credibility, authority, and ethical character of the speaker/writer.
  • Pathos — emotional connection, values, and imagination elicited in the audience.
  • Logos — logical reasoning, evidence, and structure of the argument.

Ethos — Building Credibility

Ethos persuades by establishing trustworthiness. Techniques include citing qualifications, demonstrating fairness, using measured tone, and acknowledging complexity.

Example: "As a public-health researcher with ten years of experience studying vaccination programs, I draw on multiple community trials to recommend..."

  • Use credible sources and explain your expertise.
  • Avoid overstatements and recognize limitations.

Pathos — Using Emotion Ethically

Pathos connects the audience to the topic emotionally. Effective use clarifies values and stakes; misuse can manipulate or distract.

Example: A survivor's testimony can humanize statistics, but it should be framed so the anecdote supports—not replaces—systematic evidence.

  • Use vivid but relevant examples to engage readers.
  • Balance emotion with evidence to avoid fallacies.

Logos — Structure and Evidence

Logos relies on sound reasoning: clear claims, warranted inferences, and appropriate evidence (statistics, experiments, textual analysis).

Checklist for logos: define terms, show evidence quality, connect evidence to claim, and avoid logical fallacies.

Analyzing Appeals — Quick Method

  1. Identify the claim and main supporting reasons.
  2. For each supporting sentence, label whether it appeals to ethos, pathos, or logos.
  3. Assess balance: does the author over-rely on one appeal? Is any appeal misused?
  4. Evaluate effectiveness for the intended audience and context.

Sample Analysis (Short)

Passage: "Every parent remembers the fear of a sick child. We cannot ignore the children left behind."

Analysis: The passage uses pathos (evokes parental fear) to heighten urgency, and an implicit ethos by assuming shared responsibility. Effective as a call-to-action, it should be paired with logos (data) to make policy recommendations credible.

Using Appeals in Your Writing

  • Start with logos: present clear claims and evidence.
  • Use ethos to establish trust before making bold policy claims.
  • Add pathos to illustrate consequences and engage readers, but always connect anecdotes to evidence.

Practice Prompts & Example Paragraph

Prompt: Argue whether universities should require ethics training for all new researchers.

Example paragraph: Requiring ethics training for new researchers strengthens research integrity and protects participants. Empirical studies show that structured ethics programs reduce protocol violations (Johnson et al., 2016), demonstrating logos-based support. When training is led by respected senior researchers and institutional leaders, it also builds ethos, signaling organizational commitment. Finally, case studies of harm avoided after training help stakeholders understand the human consequences, bringing pathos into alignment with evidence.

Additional Examples & Analyses

Example 1 (Ethos-heavy): "As the former director of the city's public-health department, I have overseen vaccination campaigns that increased coverage by 20% in underserved neighborhoods."

Analysis: Establishes authority and experience (ethos). Effective when supported by data; without evidence it risks appearing anecdotal.

Example 2 (Pathos-focused): "Imagine a mother who cannot afford medicine for her sick child; that image should motivate policymakers to act."

Analysis: Strong emotional pull (pathos). To persuade policy-makers, pair with logos (cost estimates, impact studies) to justify action.

Example 3 (Logos-centered): "Meta-analyses of 15 randomized trials show a mean effect size of 0.35 (95% CI: 0.20–0.50), indicating a small-to-moderate intervention effect."

Analysis: Presents statistical evidence (logos). Clarify sample, measures, and limitations so the audience can assess generalizability.

Example 4 (Misused Appeal — Fallacy): "Everyone knows this approach works — it's the only sensible choice."

Analysis: Appeals to popular opinion (bandwagon) and asserts certainty without evidence. Flag as weak reasoning and replace with concrete support.

Checklist — Evaluating Appeals

  • Is the evidence credible and appropriately cited?
  • Does the author establish credibility where needed?
  • Are emotional appeals relevant and not manipulative?
  • Are there logical fallacies undermining the argument?

Additional Examples Across Contexts

Example 1: Public Health Campaign (All Three Appeals)

Passage: "Dr. Sarah Chen, a CDC epidemiologist with 15 years of experience tracking infectious diseases, warns that vaccination rates below 85% compromise community immunity. In neighborhoods where coverage dropped, we saw preventable outbreaks affecting the most vulnerable—infants too young to vaccinate and immunocompromised seniors. Data from 12 comparable cities show that improving access through mobile clinics increased coverage by 23% within six months."

Analysis:

  • Ethos: Establishes Dr. Chen's credentials and CDC affiliation.
  • Pathos: Highlights vulnerable populations (infants, seniors) to evoke compassion and urgency.
  • Logos: Cites specific data (85% threshold, 23% increase) and causal reasoning.

Example 2: Academic Argument (Ethos-Heavy)

Passage: "As documented in three peer-reviewed meta-analyses published in leading journals, and replicated across diverse populations, the correlation between sleep deprivation and cognitive decline is robust. I acknowledge that some recent observational studies show weaker effects, but methodological limitations—small sample sizes and self-reported sleep data—reduce their reliability."

Analysis: The author builds ethos by citing authoritative sources, acknowledging counterevidence, and explaining why that evidence is less persuasive. This fairness strengthens credibility.

Example 3: Advertisement (Pathos-Driven)

Passage: "Picture your child's first day of school—the nervous excitement, the new backpack. Now imagine them confidently raising their hand, prepared and eager to learn. Our tutoring program helped 92% of families see improved grades and renewed confidence."

Analysis: Uses vivid imagery and parental emotions (pride, hope) to create connection. The statistic adds logos, but the primary appeal is emotional engagement.

Example 4: Political Speech (Balanced Appeals)

Passage: "I've served this community for two decades—first as a teacher, then as your school board member—and I understand the challenges families face. Recent state budget cuts reduced our arts funding by 40%, forcing program cancellations that affect 2,000 students. These aren't just numbers; they're our children losing opportunities to explore creativity and build confidence. I propose reallocating 3% of administrative costs to restore these programs within one year."

Analysis:

  • Ethos: Cites personal experience and community ties.
  • Pathos: Emphasizes children's lost opportunities.
  • Logos: Provides specific data (40% cut, 2,000 students, 3% reallocation) and a concrete proposal.

Example 5: Environmental Advocacy (Logos + Pathos)

Passage: "Global temperatures have risen 1.2°C since pre-industrial times, accelerating ice melt and sea-level rise. Coastal cities housing 300 million people face increased flooding risk by 2050. Beyond statistics, consider the fishing villages in Bangladesh already losing homes to storm surges—families displaced, livelihoods destroyed."

Analysis: Opens with logos (temperature data, population figures) to establish factual grounding, then shifts to pathos (displaced families) to humanize the issue and motivate action.

Example 6: Business Proposal (Ethos + Logos)

Passage: "Our firm has successfully implemented cloud migrations for 15 Fortune 500 clients, reducing IT costs by an average of 28% while improving uptime to 99.97%. We propose a phased approach: assessment (month 1), pilot migration (months 2-3), and full rollout (months 4-6), with contingency plans at each stage."

Analysis: Builds ethos through track record with major clients, then uses logos (specific metrics, structured timeline) to demonstrate competence and reduce perceived risk.
