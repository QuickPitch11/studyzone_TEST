Rhetorical Appeals — Overview

A concise overview of ethos, pathos, and logos in persuasive communication. Click "In-Depth" for the complete guide.

What are Rhetorical Appeals?

Rhetorical appeals are persuasive strategies used in writing and speaking to convince an audience. Aristotle identified three primary modes of persuasion: ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion), and logos (logic).

This is the brief overview page. For comprehensive analysis including examples, application strategies, rhetorical devices, and critical analysis techniques, use the "In-Depth" link below.

The Three Appeals

Ethos (Credibility & Trust)

What it is: Establishing the speaker's or writer's credibility, authority, and trustworthiness.

How to use it:

  • • Demonstrate expertise or credentials
  • • Show fairness and honesty
  • • Use credible sources and citations
  • • Acknowledge counterarguments
  • • Maintain professional tone

Example: "As a physician with 20 years of experience, I can confidently say that regular exercise significantly improves cardiovascular health."

Pathos (Emotion & Values)

What it is: Appealing to the audience's emotions, values, beliefs, and desires.

How to use it:

  • • Tell compelling stories or anecdotes
  • • Use vivid, descriptive language
  • • Evoke specific emotions (fear, hope, sympathy, anger)
  • • Appeal to shared values (justice, freedom, family)
  • • Use imagery and sensory details

Example: "Imagine a child going to bed hungry every night, not knowing when their next meal will come. Your donation can change that."

Logos (Logic & Reason)

What it is: Using logical reasoning, facts, statistics, and evidence to support claims.

How to use it:

  • • Present clear, logical arguments
  • • Use statistics and data
  • • Cite research and expert testimony
  • • Show cause-and-effect relationships
  • • Use analogies and examples

Example: "Studies show that 80% of students who attend tutoring sessions improve their grades by at least one letter grade within a semester."

The Rhetorical Triangle

Effective persuasion balances all three appeals, considering:

  • → Speaker/Writer (Ethos): Your credibility and character
  • → Audience (Pathos): Their emotions, values, and needs
  • → Message (Logos): The logical structure and evidence

Quick Examples in Context

Advertisement for environmental action:

  • Ethos: "According to NASA scientists..."
  • Pathos: "Think about the world we're leaving for our children."
  • Logos: "Carbon emissions have increased 40% since 1990, correlating with rising global temperatures."

Student arguing for later school start times:

  • Ethos: "As a straight-A student and student council member, I represent many students' concerns."
  • Pathos: "Imagine struggling to stay awake in class, missing crucial information because you're exhausted."
  • Logos: "Research shows teens need 8-10 hours of sleep, but 73% of high schoolers get less than 8 hours on school nights."

When to Use Each Appeal

Use Ethos When:

  • • Establishing expertise
  • • Building trust
  • • Introducing new ideas
  • • Audience is skeptical

Use Pathos When:

  • • Inspiring action
  • • Creating urgency
  • • Making memorable impact
  • • Connecting personally

Use Logos When:

  • • Proving a point
  • • Academic writing
  • • Addressing logic-focused audiences
  • • Supporting claims

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • ✗ Over-relying on one appeal: Balance is key; using only emotion or only logic weakens persuasion
  • ✗ Manipulative pathos: Exploiting emotions dishonestly damages credibility
  • ✗ False ethos: Claiming expertise you don't have or misrepresenting credentials
  • ✗ Logical fallacies: Flawed reasoning undermines logos (e.g., false cause, hasty generalizations)
  • ✗ Ignoring audience: Not considering what appeals will resonate with your specific audience

Quick Analysis Checklist

When analyzing rhetorical appeals in a text, ask:

  • ✓ What credentials or authority does the speaker/writer establish? (Ethos)
  • ✓ What emotions are being evoked? How? (Pathos)
  • ✓ What evidence, facts, or logical reasoning is used? (Logos)
  • ✓ Which appeal is most prominent? Why might that be?
  • ✓ Is the balance appropriate for the audience and purpose?
